The value of V-Bucks collapsed Sunday morning after the in-game Item Shop announced it would give everything away for free for two weeks, wiping out the life savings of at least one local fifth grader and sending shockwaves through every middle school cafeteria in the country.

"I skipped lunch money for three years," said Tucker Pembrook, 11, speaking from a beanbag he has reportedly not left since 8 AM. "I had a plan. I had a spreadsheet. My mom helped me make the spreadsheet. It had colors."

Tucker had saved 13,500 V-Bucks, a sum he described as "generational wealth." It is now worth roughly one handshake.

## The Crash

Analysts say the collapse happened faster than anyone predicted. By 8:15 AM, a single V-Buck was trading for one Pokémon card, slightly bent. By 8:45 AM, it was trading for half a chicken nugget. By 9 AM, it was trading for nothing at all, because the nuggets had all been eaten.

"This is the worst day for skin-based currency since the Great Emote Bubble," said Dr. Penny Wise, chief economist at the Institute of Pretend Money, who has never played the game but owns a very nice glider. "We saw children in the streets trying to trade V-Bucks for anything. A pencil. A Capri Sun. A single gummy worm. Nobody was buying."

Trading was briefly halted at the Westbrook Middle School lunch table at 12:10 PM after a sixth grader attempted to sell 2,000 V-Bucks for "one Dorito, any flavor" and was laughed at by a kindergartner.

> "I had a plan. I had a spreadsheet. My mom helped me make the spreadsheet. It had colors."

## Investors in Shambles

Tucker is not alone. A Chaotic News Network survey found that 94 percent of fifth graders had invested "everything" in V-Bucks, with the remaining 6 percent invested in Pokémon cards, slime, or a lemonade stand that has been closed since July.

"I told my friends to diversify," said Tucker's classmate, Ava Lindqvist, 10, who had put her savings into Robux. "They said 'what does diversify mean.' Now they know."

Bulgeet & Bibi Enterprises, LLC, has already responded to the crisis with a new offer on its website: "V-Buck Insurance," which promises to "restore your V-Bucks to full value" for a $500 gift card. The website's "Buy Now" button, like every other button on the site, does nothing.

"Very good deal," CEO Bulgeet said by phone. "Steve will explain." He then transferred our reporter to Steve, who transferred her to Steve.

## The Family Responds

Tucker's parents say they are trying to be supportive. Over the weekend they offered him $20 in actual money to make up for the loss. He refused it, calling cash "a fake currency for boomers."

"He looked at the twenty dollars like it was a rock," said his father, Glenn Pembrook. "He asked what skins it comes with. I said it doesn't come with any skins. It's money. He said, 'Then what's it for?'"

His older sister, Brooke, 15, who has never purchased a single V-Buck, has been described by the family as "unbearable," walking around the house saying "I told you so" and wearing a free skin she got five minutes after the announcement.

## What Comes Next

Experts say the market could recover once the two-week free period ends. Others are less hopeful.

"Once you've had free," said Dr. Wise, "you can never go back. That's what happened with free samples at the grocery store. Nobody has paid for a cube of cheese since 1997."

Tucker says he has learned a valuable lesson about money, risk and the importance of spending your savings while it still means something.

At press time, Tucker had cashed in the last of his V-Bucks for a single emote, a dance called "The Collapse," which he has been performing in the living room for six straight hours.
